    Headlines about a type 1 diabetes cure tend to quote a program's stage: "Phase 3", "in the clinic", "IND cleared". Stage measures how far a sponsor has pushed. It says little about what has been shown. This site therefore grades every program twice: stage, the furthest point of active development, and evidence, the highest-quality human data actually read out.1 The chart below puts the two side by side.

    Reading the chart

    {% set pre = proven|selectattr("stage", "eq", "preclinical")|list %} {% set clin = proven|rejectattr("stage", "eq", "preclinical")|list %}

    The diagonal flatters the field. {{ proven|length }} of {{ act|length }} active programs sit on it, but {{ pre|length }} of those are preclinical, where stage and evidence match only because there is no human data yet. The clinical programs with evidence that matches their stage are {% for p in clin %}{{ p.short }} ({{ p.stage|grade }}){{ ", " if not loop.last else "." }}{% endfor %} The approved ones show the limits of today's options. Lantidra uses deceased-donor islets, so supply is capped, and it needs chronic immunosuppression. Teplizumab delays or slows the disease; it does not restore insulin production.2,3

    The most advanced cell therapy is still a trade. Vertex's zimislecel is at Phase 3 stage with Phase 2-grade evidence: in the NEJM report, 10 of 12 full-dose participants were insulin-independent at one year.4 It requires chronic immunosuppression, so it swaps insulin for a different lifelong drug regimen, aimed first at patients with severe hypoglycemia.

    The "real cure" is the earliest. Immune-evasive (hypoimmune) cells are the version that would need no immunosuppression. The best evidence is Sana's UP421: surviving, insulin-producing cells without immunosuppression in a single patient, at a dose too low to replace insulin.5 Sana's scalable successor, SC451, has not dosed a patient. Vertex's VX-017 has an IND cleared and no human data.

    What has dropped out

    {{ stopped|length }} programs are discontinued and kept on record: {% for p in stopped %}{{ p.short }} ({{ p.company }}){{ ", " if not loop.last else "." }}{% endfor %} Vertex's VX-264, which put stem-cell islets inside a protective device, was safe but did not produce enough C-peptide.6 Diamyd's Phase 3 stopped for futility. Each one removes a shot on goal, which is why the count of active programs matters less than where they sit on the chart.

    Implication

    For the Cure Impact Model, this argues for treating near-term approvals as the immunosuppression-required case: real, but narrow. The scenarios in which a cure displaces meaningful device and insulin revenue all depend on immune-evasive programs moving up the chart, and the best of them currently has {{ GRADE_LABEL[GRADES[ge_ev]] }} evidence.
